Assignment 2: White Hat vs. Black Hat Views of Greenhouse Gas Emissions

Brady Bragg, James Crabtree, and Ethan Zhang

Dataset: Greenhouse Gas Emissions 1990-2018


White-Hat Write-Up

The dataset used is the Greenhouse Gas Emissions Dataset, which includes data on the CO2-equivalent greenhouse gas emissions per person by year for up to 94 countries (depending on the year) spanning from 2015 through 2024. Only data from 2015 through 2022 was used in the visualization, as 2023 and 2024 each had data for significantly fewer countries (46-48 as opposed to 84-94 for the earlier years). Including these final two years would have dramatically reduced the scope of the visualization, which could have exacerbated any missing data biases present in the dataset, as it is unknown whether roughly half of the countries from the earlier years are missing at random or not. For full transparency, the end date of 2022 is clearly and prominently communicated in both the title and tooltips of the visualization, which shows how countries' CO2-equivalent emissions per person have changed over time. Some of the countries present in the dataset did not have data available for either 2015 or 2022, so these countries were dropped so that the same time period is used for all countries visualized. (Tableau defaulted to setting such countries change in per-capita GHG emissions to zero, but since that could be misleading, implying that the change was truly zero when the actual change is unknown, the change values for these countries were set to null instead.) Since lower levels of greenhouse gas emissions are generally perceived to be a positive change, the color scale was chosen such that darker greens indicate larger drops in GHG emissions per person, while darker reds indicate larger increases. Importantly, a neutral light tan color appears not exactly in between the minimum and maximum values, but at zero, such that a neutral color corresponds with a neutral change. To further increase transparency and detail, hovering over each country on the main world map visualization opens a bar chart that shows how GHG emissions per person in that country evolved over the entire seven-year period, which adds an extra level of nuance compared to just the end minus start value, allowing users to easily explore country-level patterns throughout the relevant period. For these secondary bar chart visualizations, because GHG emissions is a quantitative, absolute value, the y-axis starts at zero so that the relative sizes of the bars do not mislead readers into thinking that the magnitudes of the changes are larger than their actual values. However, to allow for easier comparison of years within a country, the bars are given their own color scale. Finally, the tooltips communicate the exact values of the 2015 to 2022 change in greenhouse gas emissions per person to allow for more precise reading of the data compared to what the color legend might be able to depict on its own.

To create the white-hat visualization, the dataset was first opened in a Google Sheets document to perform some basic exploration of the dataset (answering questions such as "What do the columns mean?" and "How many unique countries are there per year?"). Then, a Tableau workbook was created to design the primary visualization in one sheet and the secondary bar charts in another. Dealing with the fact that the dataset treated the year as an attribute as opposed to a dimension and creating the appropriate filters and calculated fields to address that concern took the most time. In total, it took one person roughly three hours to complete this visualization. AI tools were not used to create this visualization.

Black-Hat Write-Up

The black-hat visualization uses the same Greenhouse Gas Emissions Dataset as the white-hat visualization, but it only uses the "OECD" aggregate row, which gives the CO2-equivalent greenhouse gas emissions per person for OECD member countries as a whole from 2015 through 2024. The goal of the visualization is to convince readers that OECD countries are making rapid, steady progress on climate change. In reality, OECD emissions per person fell from about 11.9 to 10.3 tonnes, a decline of 13.6%, which is real but far more modest than the chart makes it appear.

The first manipulation is in the data transformation. Every value was divided by the 2015 value and multiplied by 100 to create an "emissions index" where 2015 equals 100. Indexing is a common technique, which makes it look trustworthy, but here it hides the original units, so readers never learn how large emissions are or that the values are per person (a per-person decline is not the same as a decline in total emissions, since the population of OECD countries grew over this period). The years 2023 and 2024 were also kept, even though the white-hat visualization excluded them because so many countries were missing. In the dataset, the United States, Mexico, and Chile have no figures for either year, Korea is also missing in 2024, and every OECD-wide value is flagged as an estimate. These less reliable years also happen to be the two lowest points on the chart, so including them makes the decline look larger and more recent.

The second manipulation is in the marks and encodings. The y-axis runs from 84 to 104 instead of starting at zero, so the 13.6% decline drops the line through about two-thirds of the chart's height, exaggerating the change. To make this harder to notice, the axis extends slightly above 100 so the line does not start at the very top, and the area under the line is shaded down to the bottom of the axis. Shaded areas are usually read as starting at zero, so the shrinking area suggests a large share of emissions has disappeared.

The third manipulation is in the titles and labels. The title "OECD Climate Progress" tells readers what to conclude before they look at the data and implies the decline came from deliberate climate policy. The y-axis is labeled only "Emissions Index," never mentioning that the values are per person or estimated. The x-axis only labels every other year, and 2020 is one of the unlabeled years, so readers are not prompted to connect that year's sharp drop to the COVID-19 pandemic, and the 2021 rebound reads as a temporary setback. Each of these choices appears in legitimate charts, which is what makes the visualization subtly misleading: no single part is false, but all together they make a very modest decline look like a dramatic success. AI tools were used in making this visualization, similar to Lab 8.


Extra Credit

For extra credit, an interactive axis toggle was added to the black-hat visualization. The button above redraws the chart with an animated transition from its truncated 84 to 104 y-axis to an honest axis that starts at zero, and clicking it again restores the black-hat version. With the zero-based axis, the same data that appears to plunge becomes a small decline, which helps visualize the black-hat technique instead of just reading about it.


Development Process

The work was split among the three team members. James Crabtree created the white-hat visualization in Tableau and wrote its write-up, Ethan Zhang created the black-hat visualization in D3.js, and Brady Bragg wrote the black-hat write-up and references, added the extra credit axis toggle, and polished the webpage. In total, the project took roughly eight people-hours: about three hours for the white-hat visualization, about three hours for the black-hat visualization, and about two hours for the black-hat write-up, extra credit, and webpage polish. For the white-hat visualization, the most time was spent creating the filters and calculated fields needed to handle the year being treated as an attribute instead of a dimension, as described in the white-hat write-up. The black-hat visualization was built starting from the Lab 8 file structure and went through several revisions to make its techniques more subtle, such as removing value labels that would have revealed the true size of the decline. For the black-hat write-up, the most time went into checking the dataset to confirm the numbers used to describe each technique, such as the size of the decline and which countries were missing in 2023 and 2024.
